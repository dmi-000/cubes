The distortion is not small. In Cayley coordinates the segments measure D1 0.26, D2 0.33, B 0.72, A 7.48, C 134.37 — arc C is five hundred times arc D1. Drawn to scale, four of the five lines would be dots. Station counts are the honest axis instead: C has the most classes on it because it is the longest arc, and the map spaces them evenly the way a subway map does.

The one crossing that is not an interchange

The carrier lines of C and D1 meet exactly, at Cayley (37/23, 2/23, −29/46). But that point sits at s = −6.84 on C and t = 0.391 on D1, outside both 727 ranges, and the count there is 725. So the lines form a connected network while the arcs do not — which is exactly why the component count stays at four rather than collapsing. The map draws the crossing greyed and unlinked, the transit convention for two lines that share no station.

Chamber walls as zone boundaries

A chamber wall is not a property of one arc. It is a surface in configuration space, so different arcs cross the same wall at different stations — which is exactly what a fare-zone boundary does on a transit map, and the analogy turns out to be literal rather than decorative.

Counting the W4 surfaces each arc meets inside its 727 range: arc A crosses 40, arc B 32, arc C 36. Most are private to one line, but they do overlap — 2 shared by A and B, 2 by A and C, 4 by B and C, and one crossed by all three: the wall where the free cube's face plane passes through the base triple point (1, 7/11, −1/11). It meets arc A at s = 2.5997 (between stations 403 and 2741), arc B at s = 0.5753 (just past 1614, close to that terminus), and arc C at s = 11.6614 (between 1785 and 1930). The three crossing points are genuinely distinct, 29 to 70 apart in Cayley space.

A caution recorded because it briefly looked like a deeper coincidence: the catalogue reports this as two walls, indices 131 and 168, firing at the identical parameter on every arc. Those two triple points are antipodal, and since the cubes are centrally symmetric a face plane through p puts its opposite face through −p. It is one wall listed twice, not a doubly degenerate one.

What the map is missing

Only the one shared wall is drawn. Each arc crosses 32 to 40 W4 surfaces and far more W3 ones, all known exactly as roots of the wall equations, and drawing them all would bury the lines. Every arc also has 72 chart copies under the free cube's own rotations and the base's C₃; one representative per class is drawn. And the fourth line, D, is really two arcs whose extents are 1/4 and 5/16 — the shortest in the network, drawn longest here because an interchange needs room.
